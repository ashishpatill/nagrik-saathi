Supersedes the hackathon-scoped v1 plan.

Framing shift: v1 was a hackathon artifact — small scope, BYOK SerpApi, no automation, no auth, stateless. Those were time and rules constraints, not product decisions. v2 removes them. The only real constraint that remains is the one that matters: we do not have government internal APIs, server access, or proprietary data. Everything below is what is possible inside that boundary, done properly.
Where v1 actually ended (verified against the repo):
  • Eight WebMCP tools registered through document.modelContext, with readOnlyHint / untrustedContentHint annotations, a polyfill and local fallback, and an inspector mode at /?inspector=1.
  • Eight languages, deterministic regex extraction, in-browser PDF and OCR processing, scam signals, a review-only letter drafter, and a human-reviewed portal registry.
  • Cases persisted locally in IndexedDB; approval gates already guard reminder and family-brief export; vitest covers the trust rules. A refresh already keeps the workspace — the v2 gap is encryption, cross-device durability, and a portable exit.
  • Stage 0 hardening is largely present in the working tree: CSP/HSTS/frame-ancestors/Referrer-Policy, per-IP rate limiting, checksum-aware redaction, pinned dependencies, public/robots.txt, llms.txt, sitemap.xml, and the eight-language API enum.
  • At v1 close, still missing: CI audit/SBOM gate, auth, encrypted cloud case store, MCP server, process playbooks, portal-health fleet, offline PWA, and A2A. The current working tree has since added the CI/SBOM gate, the twelve-tool registry, the playground, and a local MCP server; see §9.2 and §9.3.

1. What maximum value without government access actually means

The public web already exposes enormous amounts of what citizens need. Forms, notices, helplines, office addresses, required-document lists, fee schedules, status-check pages, grievance portals, RTI formats, and the logic of nearly every government workflow are all publicly visible. What is not public is: authenticated session state, internal databases, payment rails, and the right to write to government systems.

So the honest ceiling for Phase 1 is this: Nagrik Saathi becomes the most capable public-web civic operating system in India — an agent-driven layer that understands government processes, monitors them continuously, preserves citizen state across failures, guides users through every step, and hands them a verified path to the official action. It cannot click submit on a government server. It can make sure that when the citizen does, everything is ready, correct, and recoverable.

The value proposition in one sentence: When any Indian government portal fails, Nagrik Saathi is where the citizen goes — because it already knows their case, their documents, their deadlines, and the exact official step they need, and it never loses any of it.

1.1 The boundary, drawn precisely

LayerPhase 1 accessWhat it enables

Citizen's device Local processing with explicit consent OCR, extraction, drafts, scam checks, IndexedDB cases
Opt-in encrypted store Only after passkey identity and consent Cross-device cases, reminders, export and delete
Government internal systems None in Phase 1 No login, no form submission, no payment, no write access

The test for every feature: does it help the citizen act correctly on the official system, without pretending to be the official system?

What we deliberately do not build in Phase 1

  • Anything that requires logging into a government account on the citizen's behalf with write access.
  • Payment processing or challan submission.
  • Aadhaar number collection or eKYC handling. Identity is passkey-only; Aadhaar linking is a Phase 2 conversation with MeitY's own eKYC rails.
  • A central database of citizen records. Per-user encrypted stores or nothing.

2. The full product vision — nine capability domains

v1 had one domain: notice analysis. v2 has nine, each with a defined user journey, data model, and agent-facing tool surface. Nine is deliberately wide, but it is not a super-app: each domain exists to make the citizen's encounter with an official process survivable and correct.

Domain 1 — Notice & document intelligence

Beyond PDF, text, and photo ingestion: OCR for handwritten and degraded scans, multi-notice batch processing, deadline extraction with calendar integration, fee and penalty computation, and cross-referencing a notice against the relevant Act or Rule section. The LLM extracts; deterministic validators (regex, checksums, section-number lookups against a maintained legal corpus) confirm. Nothing an LLM asserts about an amount or date is shown without a pattern match behind it.

Domain 2 — Scam & fraud defense

A continuously updated pattern library: known scam templates, coercive-language classifiers, payment-demand anomaly detection (amounts that do not match official schedules), sender-impersonation checks (domain similarity to official portals), and a report loop where users submit scam notices that feed the library after human review. This becomes a public good — the pattern database is open, versioned, and citable.

Domain 3 — Process guidance engine

For every major government workflow, a structured playbook: steps, required documents, fees, typical timelines, common rejection reasons, and the exact portal URL at each step. Playbooks are maintained as versioned YAML/JSON content — not hardcoded — so the community, and later government partners, can contribute.

Seed set (15): e-Visa, passport, PAN, driving licence, GST registration, income-tax filing, RTI, labour complaint, property registration, birth certificate, death certificate, electricity connection, ration card, consumer complaint, municipal grievance. Aadhaar update is a guidance-only addition: explain the process, never collect the number.

Domain 4 — Portal health & resilience monitor

A background fleet that probes every registry domain on a schedule: uptime, latency, TLS validity, whether the login page renders, whether key form pages return 200. Results feed a public dashboard and per-user banners. When IRCTC or the e-Visa site is down, Nagrik Saathi tells the citizen before they waste an hour. Cached last-known-good snapshots of critical pages (helplines, office lists, fee tables) serve as fallback content with staleness timestamps.

Domain 5 — Case & state preservation

The single biggest v1 gap — but not the one the demo suggested. v1's IndexedDB store already survives a refresh on one device. What it never had was encryption, cross-device durability, a portable exit, or a case that survives the citizen switching phones. Every interaction becomes a durable, encrypted case: uploaded documents (field-level encrypted), extracted fields, generated briefs, checklists, drafted letters, portal links opened, health alerts received. Cases are exportable as encrypted files (the zero-knowledge escape hatch) and deletable with one action.

Domain 6 — Drafting & response studio

Review-only letter and application drafting in English, Hindi, and Marathi, with tone controls (formal, polite-firm, complaint), section-aware templates pulled from the process playbooks, and a compare-against-official-format checker that flags missing mandatory fields before the citizen ever opens the government site.

Domain 7 — Grievance & escalation routing

A mapped escalation graph: which grievance channel handles which problem (CPGRAMS, state portals, helplines, RTI, consumer forum, Lokpal), with pre-filled draft complaints and tracking-number capture so the citizen can log outcomes back into their case.

Domain 8 — Multilingual & accessibility layer

Bhashini-powered translation for all output, Indic-script layout rules, screen-reader-complete UI, and a voice-first mode for users who cannot read comfortably — which, in rural India, is a large share of the people most harmed by broken portals.

Domain 9 — Community & content engine

The existing X/content muscle, pointed inward: guides, threads, and videos teaching citizens how to use each portal and how to use Nagrik Saathi with their own agents. Content is both growth and proof.

3. Security-first architecture

In v1, security was a section. In v2 it is the spine every other section hangs from. Zero-trust from edge to browser, every trust boundary named, every invariant testable.

3.1 Threat model

  • T1 — Prompt injection via notice content. All pasted or uploaded text is untrusted input. System prompts forbid following embedded instructions. Tool descriptions carry trust-level annotations. Red-team this before launch. No model-supplied URLs: portal links come only from the reviewed registry.
  • T2 — Secret exposure. No API key in client bundles. BYOK remains the public default; a server-side vault or sealed environment is the operator opt-in. Automatic rotation, per-environment separation.
  • T3 — Session replay on future automation. Disposable browser profiles, single-use sessions, no cookie reuse across users or across time.
  • T4 — Data-at-rest breach. Field-level encryption, per-user keys derived from passkey identity, envelope encryption for the key store.
  • T5 — Supply chain. Lockfile integrity, Dependabot/Renovate, pinned CI actions, SBOM on every release, provenance attestations.
  • T6 — Agent abuse of WebMCP/MCP tools. Per-origin rate limits, allowlists for side-effect tools, full invocation logging.
  • T7 — PII leakage through logs or LLM providers. Redaction pipeline before any text leaves the device or reaches a provider; log only hashes and redacted forms.

3.3 Concrete controls

  1. CSP in next.config.ts: strict script-src, no inline eval outside the WebMCP polyfill path, frame-ancestors 'none'. Current Stage 0 allows 'unsafe-inline' for Next.js hydration and 'wasm-unsafe-eval' for Tesseract; tightening to nonces is a later hardening item, not a reason to pretend the current policy is strict.
  2. Subresource Integrity on every third-party script. Prefer bundling over CDNs where the dependency allows it.
  3. Shared Zod schemas between client and API routes — one definition of valid input.
  4. Rate limiting per IP and per key on all dynamic endpoints, with jittered backoff.
  5. PII redaction: Aadhaar via 12-digit plus Verhoeff check, PAN via format and checksum, cards via Luhn, OTPs via pattern. Apply it to tool logs and anything leaving the device. Log only redacted versions.
  6. Append-only hash-chained audit log: timestamp, actor, tool, input hash, output hash, origin.
  7. CI gate: pnpm audit, OSV scan, CycloneDX SBOM on release, and a failing build on high-severity findings.
  8. DPDP Act 2023 alignment documented in docs/SAFETY.md: on-device default means minimal fiduciary exposure; any cloud feature gets purpose limitation, retention caps, a deletion endpoint, and consent capture.

3.4 Invariants every pull request is checked against

  • No secret in a client bundle, log line, error message, or commit.
  • No portal URL accepted from a model or user without registry verification.
  • No state-changing action without a visible approval gate and an audit entry.
  • No document text sent to a provider before redaction, unless the user explicitly opts in and the provider contract forbids training on it.
  • No central citizen database. Encrypted per-user stores only.
  • No claim of official status. Every output says it is guidance, not a government decision.
The non-negotiable: the no-storage, on-device default stays the default forever. Cloud features are opt-in, encrypted, and deletable. The moat is that Nagrik Saathi is the civic tool you can trust with a scam notice — never erode that for convenience.

4. Protocol layer

4.1 WebMCP — the in-browser surface

Expand from eight hackathon tools to a complete agent-operable registry. Keep document.modelContext canonical; navigator.modelContext is only a compatibility fallback. Every tool carries: JSON Schema inputs, a plain-language description, trustLevel (read-only / untrusted-content / side-effect), and a max-frequency hint.

New tools, in build order:

  • Reuse existing logic first: verify_portal_url, redact_pii, translate_brief, generate_checklist_pdf.
  • After the resilience layer: get_portal_health, lookup_process_playbook.
  • After identity and playbooks: get_case, save_case, draft_grievance, compare_against_official_format.

A tool contract should be boring and explicit:

{
  "name": "verify_portal_url",
  "description": "Check a URL against the human-reviewed registry.",
  "inputSchema": {
    "type": "object",
    "properties": { "url": { "type": "string", "format": "uri" } },
    "required": ["url"],
    "additionalProperties": false
  },
  "annotations": {
    "trustLevel": "read-only",
    "untrustedContentHint": false,
    "maxFrequency": "30/min"
  }
}

4.2 MCP server — the persistent surface

A TypeScript MCP server using the official SDK, sharing tool logic with the WebMCP surface through one codebase. Reachable from Claude Desktop, ChatGPT connectors, Cursor, and any custom harness. This is what makes Nagrik Saathi usable by agents the citizen already runs — including the ones you build.

Deployment reality: stdio for local clients, HTTP transport for remote clients, on a small always-on host. Do not pretend a Vercel serverless function is an MCP stdio server; the runtime is not a persistent process. The tool bodies in src/lib/* are already framework-agnostic, so the server is an adapter, not a rewrite.

4.3 A2A — cross-agent coordination

Not day one, but the tool registry is designed so each tool can later be exposed as an A2A Agent Card with signed identity. When MeitY's CoRover platform or a ministry agent needs to ask "does this citizen have an open grievance?", an A2A-compatible Nagrik Saathi answers without exposing internals. agent-card.json publishes when the server is stable.

Keep AI answer engines allowed and training crawlers disallowed, as the current robots.txt does. The distinction matters: blocking the assistant that helps a citizen is a self-inflicted wound; opting out of model training is a legitimate choice.

5. Resilience and state-preservation engine

5.1 Portal health fleet

Scheduled probes (Vercel cron or an external scheduler) hitting every registry domain: HTTP status, TLS chain, render check on login and key form pages, latency percentiles. Public dashboard. Per-user contextual banners: "IRCTC login is currently returning errors — your Tatkal window may be affected."

5.2 Cached snapshots

Periodically refreshed text snapshots plus structured metadata (helplines, addresses, fees, required documents) per registry domain. If the live site is unreachable, serve last-known-good with a visible staleness timestamp. This is the direct answer to "the server went down" — Nagrik Saathi degrades gracefully instead of dying with it. Never hide the timestamp, and never let a stale fee table look current.

5.3 Offline-first PWA

A service worker caching the static layer, playbooks, and registry. Core flows — understand a notice, check scam signals, read a playbook — work with zero connectivity after first visit. For rural and low-bandwidth users this is not a nice-to-have; it is the product. Cache versions are explicit, so a bad deploy cannot silently poison the offline copy.

5.4 Durable case store

Server-side, field-level encrypted, keyed to passkey identity. Cross-device sync. Encrypted-file export. One-click delete. The citizen's case survives every failure mode: browser crash, portal crash, Nagrik Saathi deploy, even the citizen switching phones.

5.5 Failure-mode contract

FailureWhat the citizen sees
Government portal returns 5xx or times out Live health banner, last-known-good snapshot with timestamp, official helpline, and the exact official step to retry later
No connectivity PWA serves the cached playbook, registry, and previously analyzed case; new analysis waits
Browser crash or refresh Local IndexedDB case resumes; no re-upload, no re-typing
Citizen switches devices Opt-in encrypted sync, or an encrypted export/import file as the zero-knowledge path
Nagrik Saathi itself has an outage Static layer and cached registry remain available; dynamic tools fail with a clear, honest message rather than a blank screen

6. Identity, encryption, and the DPDP posture

6.1 Default: no identity

The on-device, no-account flow remains the entry point. Paste a notice, get analysis. Zero friction, zero data retained. This is how you reach the citizen who will never create an account.

6.2 Opt-in: passkey identity

WebAuthn via @simplewebauthn/server (or Keycloak 26.4+ if the deployment needs a full identity provider). Edge-verifiable signatures, no central password store, phishing-resistant by construction. This unlocks the case store, cross-device sync, and future automation consent.

6.3 Encryption model

  • Field-level encryption for stored documents and extracted PII.
  • Per-user data keys, wrapped by a master key held in a vault — not in the application database.
  • Passkey-derived wrapping keys for the client-held side where applicable.
  • Envelope encryption throughout, so a single database leak yields ciphertext.
Passkeys authenticate; they do not encrypt by themselves. If the wrapping key is derived server-side after a passkey assertion, the server can decrypt. Be precise in the product language: call it "encrypted at rest, access-controlled by passkey" unless the WebAuthn PRF extension (or a client-derived key) keeps the wrapping key off the server entirely. Do not market it as zero-knowledge until that is literally true.

6.4 DPDP Act 2023

On-device default: minimal fiduciary exposure, documented. Cloud features: consent capture at opt-in, purpose limitation stated in plain language, retention caps with automatic purge, a breach-notification runbook, and a public deletion endpoint. docs/SAFETY.md carries the full mapping before any cloud feature ships.

7. The agent-native surface and the moat

No other Indian civic-tech project has this harness and multi-agent background. v2 makes that the centerpiece, not a side note.

7.1 What ships

  • WebMCP registry (12+ tools) live on the site.
  • MCP server in production, documented, with an AGENTS.md contract file.
  • Example agent workflows published: "read my electricity bill, check it for scams, draft my grievance, find the right portal" as one orchestrated run.
  • A public tool playground where anyone can try the tools from a browser.

7.2 What agent-native actually buys the citizen

  • No new app to learn. The citizen uses the assistant they already trust, in the language they already use.
  • Composable help. Notice analysis, scam checks, playbooks, and drafting become steps an agent can chain, instead of four separate screens.
  • Consent at the boundary. The agent can prepare; the citizen still approves anything with a side effect. That is the line that makes agentic help trustworthy.

7.3 The moat, stated plainly

Competitors can copy a UI. They cannot copy a year of community trust, an open protocol surface with real agent adopters, a scam-pattern library fed by real users, and content that ranks when people search "IRCTC down" or "e-Visa rejected." The moat is the network of agents and humans already routed through Nagrik Saathi — and that compounds. The code is copyable by design; the registry's human review, the safety brand, and the distribution are not.

8. Phase 2 — government integration

Phase 2 opens only when Phase 1 has receipts: users, uptime data, a clean security audit, and public agent adoption. The pitch is not "replace your portals." It is: become the open protocol layer that sits in front of what exists.

8.1 What changes with government access

  • MCP servers hosted inside or beside ministry infrastructure, reading authoritative data directly instead of scraping public pages.
  • WebMCP registrations injected into official portal pages — the citizen's live session, their real state, agent-readable.
  • A2A between Nagrik Saathi agents and ministry agents for cross-department checks (tax clearance before visa, address verification before passport).
  • Passkey auth federated with the government's own identity rails.
  • Write-capable, human-gated automation on official sessions — the thing Phase 1 explicitly refuses.

8.2 The procurement argument

  • Target NeGD under MeitY — they run UMANG and DigiLocker and issued the August 2026 AI-agent tender.
  • Frame as a DPI extension in the UPI/Aadhaar lineage: open protocol, private implementers, no vendor lock-in.
  • Offer a 90-day reversible pilot on the single most-complained-about surface: e-Visa.
  • Publish everything open-source except the audit report and any government data. Openness is the anti-corruption proof.
  • Lead with security: a FedRAMP-style MCP authorization framework, an NSA-CISA June 2026 compliance matrix, immutable audit trails — before they ask.

9.1 What not to do

  • No write-automation before the Stage 7 audit. Irreversible actions behind an unaudited system is how products get banned and founders get noticed for the wrong reasons.
  • No Aadhaar/PAN collection in Stages 0–5. The on-device default is the moat.
  • Do not block AI search crawlers. Block training crawlers if you want; do not accidentally block the assistants that help citizens.
  • Do not build a central citizen database. Encrypted per-user stores, or nothing.
  • Do not become a super-app. Nine domains is already wide; depth beats breadth. Pick the three that carry the promise — notice intelligence, resilience, and state preservation — and make them undeniable before adding a tenth.

10. Risks and honest limitations

A plan that lists only upside is a pitch, not a plan. These are the ways Nagrik Saathi fails, and the early signals that should change the plan.

10.2 Legal and platform

  • Terms of service. Government portals generally prohibit automated access. Read-only caching of public pages is a gray area most sites tolerate; form-filling is not. Legal review before Stage 8 is mandatory, not optional.
  • IT Act Section 43. Unauthorized access to a computer system can attract penalties. Even read-only scraping at high volume could be framed as access without authorization. Rate limiting and politeness are technical mitigations; a legal opinion is the real one.
  • DPDP Act. If any cloud feature stores personal data, you become a Data Fiduciary with consent, purpose-limitation, and breach-notification duties. The on-device default avoids this; the opt-in path triggers it.
  • SerpApi terms. BYOK shifts key custody to users, but verify that SerpApi's terms cover caching and filtering results in a public product, and that BYOK distribution is permitted.
  • WebMCP is experimental. The API can change under you. The three-tier runtime (native, polyfill, local fallback) is the mitigation; treat native support as progressive enhancement, not a dependency.
  • Accessibility compliance. If the product is used by or with a government body, GIGW and WCAG expectations arrive with the contract. UX4G alignment is cheaper before the audit than after.

10.3 Technical

  • OCR is not reliable enough to trust blindly. Tesseract on low-quality photos of Hindi or Marathi notices will misread digits. A confidently wrong amount is worse than a failure. Show the source snippet, show confidence, and never hide the original.
  • LLM hallucination. The model may invent a fee, a section number, or a deadline. Deterministic extraction and registry lookups stay authoritative; the model explains and drafts, it does not certify.
  • Portal HTML is a moving target. Health probes built on brittle selectors break silently. Probe human-visible signals and alert on probe failure, not just on a 200 response.
  • PWA cache staleness and poisoning. Versioned caches, integrity checks for snapshots, and a visible "from cache" state. A stale offline fee table is a liability.
  • Key management is hard. Passkeys do not encrypt by themselves; the PRF path is not universal. Ship the simpler, honest "encrypted at rest, passkey access" model before claiming zero-knowledge.
  • Read-only automation still gets blocked. CAPTCHAs, bot detection, and IP bans are normal. Budget for failure, degrade to guidance, and never promise uptime you do not control.
  • Cost and latency. On-device processing stays first; cloud LLM calls are for what genuinely cannot run locally. A civic tool that costs money per notice has a hard ceiling in India.

10.4 Strategic

  • You are a band-aid, not a cure. The root causes — procurement incentives, fragmented ownership, lowest-bid culture — are institutional. The product absorbs citizen pain; it does not fix the system. Be honest about this in every pitch. A facilitator that claims to "solve" e-Visa sounds like every vendor the committee has already rejected. One that says "we make the broken hour survivable and we publish the uptime data that proves it's broken" sounds like infrastructure.
  • Single-maintainer risk. This is a one-person project. Before any government conversation, it must look like an institution: pinned dependencies, CI, a second maintainer or a documented handover, and a roadmap someone else could execute. Bureaucrats fund survivable projects, not heroes.
  • Hackathon-origin code. The codebase is clean and tested by hackathon standards, but the gap between demo-grade and audit-grade is real: no CI, no error monitoring, no structured logging until recently. Budget Stage 0 as a genuine hardening sprint, not a week of polish.
  • The registry is the liability. A wrong entry in official-portals.ts is the single most damaging failure possible: the scam-checker itself pointing users at a scam. The health-check cron handles re-verification naturally; the human review is what actually protects it.
  • Competition will come. If the facilitator layer shows traction, well-funded players (including the CoRover platform itself) can copy the idea. The defensible parts are the registry's human-verified quality, the safety posture as a public brand, and the open-source community around the protocol layer — not the code.
  • Distribution is not guaranteed. Agent adoption may stay flat; content requires consistency; government sales cycles are long. Do not build a company that only works if one ministry says yes. Phase 1 must be useful on its own, to real citizens, without anyone's permission.

10.5 Pre-registered kill criteria

Write these down before the work starts, so the decision is not made by sunk cost:

  • If Phase 1 has no measurable weekly use and no third-party agent adoption after six months, stop expanding domains and focus on one wedge.
  • If both WebMCP and the MCP server fail to attract third-party agent usage, the "agent-native" thesis is wrong; pivot to a human-first resilience product.
  • If legal review says read-only automation is not defensible for the target portals, drop Stage 8 and stay guidance-only. That is still a valuable product.
  • If the audit finds unfixable high-risk issues in the case store, ship export-only local mode and delay cloud sync indefinitely.
  • If a registry entry causes a user harm, freeze expansion and re-verify the entire registry before anything else ships.
What success looks like: A citizen whose e-Visa portal is down opens Nagrik Saathi, sees the outage before wasting an hour, gets the cached checklist and the exact documents they need, receives the correct official link and helpline, and finds their case still there when they switch phones. No login to a government system. No submission on their behalf. No lost work. That is the product.
